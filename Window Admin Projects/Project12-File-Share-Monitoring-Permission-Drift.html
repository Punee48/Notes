Project 12 — File Share Monitoring & Permission Drift Detection

The share is online. The share is reachable. The share is also readable by people who shouldn't be able to read it.

Every night, a batch job drops sensitive account statements onto a file share for downstream processing. Today's project checks two very different things about every critical share: is it actually there and reachable, and — just as important — do its permissions still match what was approved, or has something quietly drifted.

\\SRV-FILE01\StatementsDrop — online, permissions match
\\SRV-FILE01\StatementsDrop — "Everyone" has Read access
\\SRV-FILE02\BatchArchive — share missing entirely

01 The Production Story

A file share that was locked down correctly six months ago... isn't anymore.
Thursday — A Routine Question From Compliance

Compliance asks a simple question during a routine review: "Who currently has access to the StatementsDrop share where nightly account statements land before processing?" Priya pulls it up and freezes. Alongside the two service accounts that are supposed to have access, the built-in Everyone group somehow has Read permission on the share. Nobody remembers changing it. The best guess: someone troubleshooting an access issue months ago added a broad permission "just to test," and it was never removed.

It turns out this share held read access open to every authenticated user on the domain for an unknown period of time — a real, reportable exposure of sensitive customer data, discovered by luck during an unrelated compliance question rather than by any monitoring.

"This is a different flavor of what we caught with local admin accounts," Priya says, "but just as serious — maybe worse, since this one's about data exposure, not just system access. I want two checks for every share we care about: first, confirm the share actually exists and is reachable, because a share silently disappearing breaks the nightly batch job just as badly. Second — and this is the one that really matters — confirm both the share-level and the underlying folder's NTFS permissions still match what we approved, and flag anything that's drifted."

This project introduces a genuinely two-layer permission model: Windows file shares are protected by BOTH share-level permissions and NTFS folder permissions independently, and the effective access someone actually has is the more restrictive of the two — which is exactly why both need checking, not just one.

02 Business Case

Business Requirement

Ensure critical file shares remain reachable and that their permissions never silently drift from an approved baseline.

Customer Requirement

Compliance and Security want continuous assurance that sensitive data shares aren't exposed beyond their intended audience.

Banking Scenario

Shares carrying account statements, batch files, and backup data are prime targets for both accidental over-exposure and real data-loss risk.

Share permissions are set once at creation time and essentially never reviewed again unless a specific question prompts it.

Problems With Current Process

Permission drift from ad-hoc troubleshooting changes can persist indefinitely, completely unnoticed.

Business Challenges

Share-level and NTFS-level permissions are two separate, independent layers — both must be checked, not just one.

Overly broad read access to sensitive customer data is a serious, directly reportable data exposure risk in a bank.

Why Automation Is Required

Only automation can compare live permissions against baseline consistently across every critical share, on a real schedule.

A PowerShell script checking share reachability, share-level ACLs (Get-SmbShareAccess), and NTFS ACLs (Get-Acl) against an approved baseline.

Project Scope

Permission drift on sensitive shares gets caught within days through routine monitoring, not discovered by chance during an unrelated conversation.

03 New Concepts (Building on Projects 01–11)

You already know Compare-Object and remote queries. Here's what's new for file shares and the two-layer permission model.
Get-SmbShare & Get-SmbShareAccess

What it is: Get-SmbShare lists SMB (file) shares configured on a server; Get-SmbShareAccess returns the share-level permissions (who can access the share itself, and at what level — Full, Change, Read) for a specific share.

Why it's used today: This is the FIRST of the two permission layers — the "front door" permission controlling who can even connect to the share at all, independent of what the underlying files and folders allow.

Get-Acl — NTFS Folder Permissions

What it is: Returns the NTFS (file system) Access Control List for a folder — the SECOND, independent permission layer, controlling what someone can actually do once they're past the share-level door, at the file system level itself.

Why it's used today: Share-level permissions alone don't tell the whole story — the story's Everyone group finding could have been at either layer, or both, which is exactly why a real audit has to check both independently.

Production nuance: A user's actual effective access is always the MORE RESTRICTIVE of the share-level and NTFS-level permissions combined — a share permission of "Full Control" means nothing if the NTFS permission underneath only allows "Read."

Test-Path Against a UNC Path

What it is: Test-Path "\\SRV-FILE01\StatementsDrop" works exactly like testing a local path, but against a network share path (UNC — Universal Naming Convention) — a simple, direct way to confirm a share is actually reachable right now.

Why it's used today: This directly answers Priya's first ask: is the share there and reachable at all, before we even get to checking its permissions.

Comparing ACL Entries Against a Baseline

What it is: Both Get-SmbShareAccess and Get-Acl return a collection of access rules, each naming an account/group and a permission level. Comparing "what's actually there" against "what's approved" is the same fundamental pattern as Projects 02 and 11 — Compare-Object or an explicit loop checking for anything unexpected.

Why it's used today: This is the check that would have caught the story's incident — an Everyone entry appearing in either ACL that isn't in the approved baseline is exactly the kind of drift this comparison is built to surface.

The recurring lesson across security-adjacent projects (11 and 12): access control in Windows is rarely a single gate — it's usually layered, and a security check that only examines one layer can miss a real exposure sitting in the other.

04 Architecture

Reachability gates everything else — a share that's gone is a different problem (an outage) from a share that's reachable but over-permissioned (an exposure) — and both branches matter independently.

06 The Script — Test-ShareCompliance.ps1

Scripts\Test-ShareCompliance.ps1 
<#
.SYNOPSIS
    File Share Monitoring & Permission Drift Detection - ABC Bank Production Support
.DESCRIPTION
    Confirms critical file shares are reachable, then checks BOTH
    share-level and NTFS-level permissions against an approved baseline,
    flagging any account present that isn't explicitly approved.
#>

# ============================================================
# SECTION 1: CONFIGURATION
# ============================================================

$SharesBaseline = Get-Content -Path "C:\ShareMonitor\Config\shares-baseline.json" -Raw | ConvertFrom-Json

$TimeTag   = Get-Date -Format 'yyyyMMdd_HHmmss'
$LogFile   = "C:\ShareMonitor\Logs\ShareCheck_$TimeTag.log"
$ReportCSV = "C:\ShareMonitor\Reports\ShareCheck_$TimeTag.csv"

function Write-Log {
    param([string]$Message, [string]$Level = "INFO")
    $Stamp = Get-Date -Format "yyyy-MM-dd HH:mm:ss"
    $Entry = "[$Stamp] [$Level] $Message"
    Add-Content -Path $LogFile -Value $Entry
    Write-Host $Entry
}

# ============================================================
# SECTION 2: PER-SHARE COMPLIANCE CHECK
# ============================================================

function Test-ShareCompliance {
    param(
        [string]$ServerName,
        [string]$ShareName,
        [string]$LocalPath,
        [string[]]$ApprovedAccounts
    )

    $Result = [PSCustomObject]@{
        ServerName          = $ServerName
        ShareName           = $ShareName
        Reachable            = $false
        ShareLevelFindings  = ""
        NtfsLevelFindings   = ""
        ComplianceStatus    = "UNKNOWN"
        CheckedAt           = Get-Date -Format "yyyy-MM-dd HH:mm:ss"
    }

    $UncPath = "\\$ServerName\$ShareName"

    # ---- CHECK 1: IS THE SHARE ACTUALLY REACHABLE? ----
    if (-not (Test-Path -Path $UncPath)) {
        Write-Log "$UncPath is UNREACHABLE" "ERROR"
        $Result.ComplianceStatus = "CRITICAL - UNREACHABLE"
        return $Result                            # No point checking permissions on a share that isn't there
    }
    $Result.Reachable = $true

    try {
        # ---- CHECK 2: SHARE-LEVEL PERMISSIONS (LAYER 1) ----
        $ShareAccess = Invoke-Command -ComputerName $ServerName -ArgumentList $ShareName -ScriptBlock {
            param($Share)
            Get-SmbShareAccess -Name $Share | Select-Object -ExpandProperty AccountName
        }

        $ShareFindings = $ShareAccess | Where-Object { $_ -notin $ApprovedAccounts }
        $Result.ShareLevelFindings = ($ShareFindings -join "; ")

        # ---- CHECK 3: NTFS-LEVEL PERMISSIONS (LAYER 2) ----
        $NtfsAccess = Invoke-Command -ComputerName $ServerName -ArgumentList $LocalPath -ScriptBlock {
            param($Path)
            (Get-Acl -Path $Path).Access | Select-Object -ExpandProperty IdentityReference | ForEach-Object { $_.Value }
        }

        $NtfsFindings = $NtfsAccess | Where-Object { $_ -notin $ApprovedAccounts }
        $Result.NtfsLevelFindings = ($NtfsFindings -join "; ")

        # ---- FINAL VERDICT: EITHER LAYER HAVING A FINDING IS A REAL FINDING ----
        if ($ShareFindings -or $NtfsFindings) {
            $Result.ComplianceStatus = "FINDING - REVIEW REQUIRED"
            Write-Log "$UncPath : permission drift detected. Share layer: [$($Result.ShareLevelFindings)] NTFS layer: [$($Result.NtfsLevelFindings)]" "WARNING"
        }
        else {
            $Result.ComplianceStatus = "COMPLIANT"
            Write-Log "$UncPath : reachable and compliant on both permission layers" "INFO"
        }
    }
    catch {
        Write-Log "Error checking permissions on $UncPath : $($_.Exception.Message)" "ERROR"
        $Result.ComplianceStatus = "CHECK FAILED"
    }

    return $Result
}

# ============================================================
# SECTION 3: MAIN EXECUTION
# ============================================================

Write-Log "===== Starting Share Compliance Check for $($SharesBaseline.Count) share(s) =====" "INFO"

$AllResults = foreach ($Share in $SharesBaseline) {
    Test-ShareCompliance -ServerName $Share.ServerName -ShareName $Share.ShareName `
                          -LocalPath $Share.LocalPath -ApprovedAccounts $Share.ApprovedAccounts
}

$AllResults | Sort-Object ComplianceStatus -Descending | Format-Table -AutoSize -Wrap

$AllResults | Export-Csv -Path $ReportCSV -NoTypeInformation

$FindingsCount = ($AllResults | Where-Object { $_.ComplianceStatus -eq "FINDING - REVIEW REQUIRED" }).Count
if ($FindingsCount -gt 0) {
    Write-Log "$FindingsCount share(s) have permission drift requiring review" "WARNING"
}

Write-Log "===== Share Compliance Check Completed. Report: $ReportCSV =====" "INFO"

07 Line-by-Line Explanation

if (-not (Test-Path -Path $UncPath)) { ... return $Result }
Checking reachability first, before either permission layer, means a genuinely offline share reports as CRITICAL - UNREACHABLE rather than a confusing or misleading permission-check error — different problems get different, clear verdicts.
(Get-Acl -Path $Path).Access | Select-Object -ExpandProperty IdentityReference
Get-Acl returns a rich object; .Access is the actual list of permission entries, and .IdentityReference on each entry is the account or group name that entry applies to — this chain drills down from "everything about this folder's security" to just the list of names we need to compare.
$ShareFindings = $ShareAccess | Where-Object { $_ -notin $ApprovedAccounts }
The same -notin pattern from Project 11, now applied independently to both permission layers — anything present that isn't explicitly approved becomes a finding, whichever layer it's found in.
if ($ShareFindings -or $NtfsFindings) { ... "FINDING - REVIEW REQUIRED" }
Either layer alone having a finding is enough to flag the whole share — this directly reflects the real-world truth that an exposure at either layer is a real exposure, not something that needs to happen at both simultaneously to matter.
Common mistake: checking only share-level permissions and assuming NTFS permissions are automatically fine, or vice versa. Because effective access is the more restrictive of the two, a clean share-level ACL can hide an overly permissive NTFS ACL underneath it, and neither layer alone tells the complete story.

08 Script Execution Flow

StepWhat Happens
1. Reachability GateTest-Path against the UNC path confirms the share exists and responds before any permission check is attempted.
2. Share-Level CheckGet-SmbShareAccess runs remotely on the hosting server, returning exactly who has share-level access right now.
3. NTFS-Level CheckGet-Acl runs against the local folder path on that same server — a completely independent permission system from the share layer.
4. Independent ComparisonBoth layers are compared against the same approved list separately — a finding in either one is tracked and reported distinctly.
5. ReportingSame log/CSV pattern as every prior project, with both layers' findings visible side by side for a reviewer.

09 Windows Internals — In Simple English

SMB — The Protocol Behind File Shares

SMB (Server Message Block) is the network protocol Windows uses to let one machine access files and folders on another — every UNC path like \\server\share is an SMB connection under the hood.

Why Two Independent Permission Layers Exist

Share permissions control network-level access to the share itself; NTFS permissions control file-system-level access to the actual files and folders — this separation lets administrators, for instance, expose a share broadly at the network level while still tightly restricting it at the file system level, or vice versa.

How Effective Access Gets Calculated

When both layers apply, Windows computes effective access as the intersection of the two — the most restrictive permission wins, which is exactly why checking only one layer can give a false sense of security.

12 Production Best Practices

Always check both share-level and NTFS-level permissions independently — a clean result at one layer says nothing about the other.
Check reachability before permissions — an offline share and an over-permissioned share are different problems needing different responses, and conflating them into one confusing error helps nobody.
Treat any broad group (Everyone, Authenticated Users, Domain Users) on a sensitive share as an automatic finding worth extra scrutiny, even if technically present in a baseline — these deserve periodic re-justification.
Keep the approved baseline under the same change-controlled ownership as the local admin audit's allow-list from Project 11 — both are security control documents, not casual config files.

13 Interview Preparation

Q: Why check both share-level and NTFS-level permissions instead of just one?
They're independent permission systems, and a user's actual effective access is the more restrictive of the two combined — checking only one layer can completely miss an exposure sitting in the other, exactly as happened in this project's story.
Q: Why gate the permission checks behind a reachability test first?
An unreachable share is a completely different kind of problem — likely an outage or configuration issue — from a reachable share with drifted permissions, which is a security exposure; conflating the two into one ambiguous error would slow down the right response to either.
Q: How would you decide what belongs in the approved accounts baseline for a given share?
Through a deliberate, documented review with the share's actual data owner and Security — identifying exactly which service accounts and roles genuinely need access, not just capturing whatever happens to currently have it.
Q: What's a real risk in relying on this check alone?
It only catches drift that's present at the moment the script runs — anything added and removed between scheduled runs would be missed, which argues for reasonably frequent scheduling on genuinely sensitive shares.
