Project 05 — Windows Service Auto-Recovery

Don't just tell us a service died. Try to bring it back — safely, with a limit, and always tell someone.

Every project so far has detected problems. Today is the first project that fixes one automatically: a stopped critical service gets restarted with retry logic and backoff, and if it still won't come back, a real email alert goes to the on-call engineer instead of waiting for the next scheduled report.

Spooler — Auto-recovered on attempt 1
W3SVC — Recovered on attempt 3
MSSQLSERVER — Failed after 3 attempts, alert sent

01 The Production Story

Your health check (Project 01) and IIS check (Project 04) are both running on schedule now. Priya has one more request.
Thursday, 2:15 AM — On-Call Phone Rings

You're the on-call engineer this week. Your phone rings at 2:15 AM: the Print Spooler service crashed on SRV-APP02, which is blocking a batch reporting job that a downstream compliance system depends on. You RDP in, half asleep, and simply click "Start" on the service. It comes back instantly. Fixed in ninety seconds — except you were awake, checked email, and couldn't fall back asleep for an hour.

The next morning, Priya says: "That's exactly the kind of thing we shouldn't need a human awake for. Your health check already tells us a service is down — now I want it to try to fix it before waking anyone up. If a non-critical service like Spooler crashes, just restart it, log it, and let us see it in the morning report. But if something like SQL Server won't come back after a few tries, that's different — that needs a real alert, right now, to whoever's on call."

"Two rules I want baked in: never retry forever — cap it at three attempts with a short wait between each, so we're not masking a deeper problem by endlessly restarting something broken. And always log every attempt, success or failure, so the next person can see exactly what the automation already tried before they arrived."

This is the first project where the script changes production state automatically, without a human approving each action in the moment — which is exactly why the retry cap and escalation rules matter as much as the recovery logic itself.

02 Business Case

Business Requirement

Automatically recover common, low-risk service failures without waking an on-call engineer for a 90-second fix.

Customer Requirement

Ops wants self-healing for routine failures, with real alerting reserved for genuinely serious ones.

Banking Scenario

Overnight batch jobs, reporting services, and print/queue services occasionally crash for transient reasons and simply need a restart.

Current Manual Process

An on-call engineer gets paged for every stopped service, regardless of how minor or easily fixed, at any hour.

Problems With Current Process

Unnecessary sleep disruption for trivial fixes, delayed recovery for the minutes it takes a human to respond, and alert fatigue over time.

Business Challenges

Auto-restarting the wrong thing, or restarting indefinitely, can mask a deeper problem instead of surfacing it.

Risk Analysis

A service that fails to recover after a reasonable number of attempts likely has a root cause that needs a human, not more retries.

Business Impact

Faster recovery for routine failures, better sleep and morale for on-call staff, and alerts that actually mean something when they fire.

Why Automation Is Required

A human simply can't respond faster than a script that's already watching and can restart in seconds.

Solution Design

A PowerShell script with capped retry logic, a short backoff between attempts, structured logging, and an email escalation on final failure.

Project Scope

Phase 1 covers a defined list of critical services, a 3-attempt retry cap, and email alerting on exhaustion.

Expected Benefits

Most routine service failures resolve in seconds without any human involvement, while real failures still reach a person quickly.

03 New Concepts (Building on Projects 01–04)

You already know Get-Service, functions, try/catch, and logging. Here's what's new for self-healing automation.
Restart-Service (and Start-Service)

What it is: Restart-Service stops and starts a Windows service in one command; Start-Service starts a stopped one directly. Both work against remote computers with -ComputerName in newer PowerShell, or via Invoke-Command for full remote compatibility.

Why it's used today: This is the actual "fix" action — the automated equivalent of what you did by hand at 2 AM in the story.

Do-While / Do-Until Loops

What it is: Unlike foreach (which loops over a known collection), do { } while (condition) or do { } until (condition) repeats a block until a condition changes — perfect for "keep trying until it works or we hit our attempt limit."

Why it's used today: We don't know in advance how many attempts a recovery will take (1, 2, or 3) — a do-until loop naturally expresses "try, check, try again if needed, stop either way at the cap."

Retry Logic With Backoff

What it is: Instead of retrying instantly and repeatedly, we wait a short, increasing amount of time between attempts (e.g. 5 seconds, then 10, then 15) using Start-Sleep. This is called backoff.

Why it matters: An instant, repeated restart can hit the exact same failure condition every time within milliseconds; a short pause gives transient issues (like a brief resource spike) a chance to clear before the next attempt.

Send-MailMessage — Real Alerting

What it is: Sends an actual email from PowerShell through an SMTP relay — this is the real version of the "alert email would go here" comments from earlier projects.

Why it's used today: Priya wants a genuine page to the on-call engineer when auto-recovery fails, not just a line buried in tomorrow's report.

Production nuance: Enterprises typically route through an internal relay server that doesn't require a personal email password in the script, and often integrate with a paging system (e.g. PagerDuty) rather than plain email for true 24/7 alerting — email is the simplest version to learn the concept with.

ValidateSet — Constraining Parameter Input

What it is: An attribute you can add to a function's parameter that restricts it to a fixed list of allowed values, e.g. only allowing "Low", "Medium", or "High" as valid severities — PowerShell rejects anything else automatically before your code even runs.

Why it's used today: We classify each service by criticality (Critical vs Routine) — ValidateSet stops a typo like "Critcal" from silently breaking the escalation logic.

Notice the shape of the safety design: cap the retries, wait between them, log everything, and escalate to a human the moment automation has done everything it reasonably can. This pattern — try, limit, log, escalate — applies to almost any self-healing automation you'll ever build.

06 The Script — Restore-CriticalServices.ps1

Scripts\Restore-CriticalServices.ps1 
<#
.SYNOPSIS
    Windows Service Auto-Recovery - ABC Bank Production Support
.DESCRIPTION
    Detects stopped services from a configured list, attempts automatic
    restart with capped retries and backoff, and escalates via email
    only when a service fails to recover after the retry limit.
#>

# ============================================================
# SECTION 1: CONFIGURATION
# ============================================================

$ServiceList = Get-Content -Path "C:\ServiceRecovery\Config\critical-services.json" -Raw | ConvertFrom-Json

$MaxAttempts   = 3                         # Hard cap — never retry forever
$BackoffSeconds = @(5, 10, 15)               # Wait time before attempt 1, 2, 3 respectively
$AlertEmailTo   = "oncall-windowsops@abcbank.com"
$AlertEmailFrom = "automation@abcbank.com"
$SmtpServer     = "smtp-relay.abcbank.internal"   # Internal relay — no personal credentials needed

$TimeTag   = Get-Date -Format 'yyyyMMdd_HHmmss'
$LogFile   = "C:\ServiceRecovery\Logs\Recovery_$TimeTag.log"
$ReportCSV = "C:\ServiceRecovery\Reports\Recovery_$TimeTag.csv"

function Write-Log {
    param([string]$Message, [string]$Level = "INFO")
    $Stamp = Get-Date -Format "yyyy-MM-dd HH:mm:ss"
    $Entry = "[$Stamp] [$Level] $Message"
    Add-Content -Path $LogFile -Value $Entry
    Write-Host $Entry
}

# ============================================================
# SECTION 2: ESCALATION FUNCTION
# ============================================================

function Send-EscalationAlert {
    param(
        [string]$ServerName,
        [string]$ServiceName,
        [int]$AttemptsMade
    )
    $Subject = "[ALERT] $ServiceName on $ServerName failed to recover after $AttemptsMade attempts"
    $Body    = "Automated recovery could not restart $ServiceName on $ServerName. Manual investigation required. See log: $LogFile"

    try {
        Send-MailMessage -To $AlertEmailTo -From $AlertEmailFrom -Subject $Subject `
            -Body $Body -SmtpServer $SmtpServer                     # Sends the real escalation email
        Write-Log "Escalation email sent for $ServiceName on $ServerName" "WARNING"
    }
    catch {
        Write-Log "FAILED TO SEND ESCALATION EMAIL: $($_.Exception.Message)" "ERROR"   # Even the alert itself can fail — always log that too
    }
}

# ============================================================
# SECTION 3: MAIN RECOVERY FUNCTION
# ============================================================

function Restore-Service {
    param(
        [string]$ServerName,
        [string]$ServiceName,
        [ValidateSet("Critical","Routine")]][string]$Criticality   # Only these two values are ever accepted
    )

    $Result = [PSCustomObject]@{
        ServerName    = $ServerName
        ServiceName   = $ServiceName
        Criticality   = $Criticality
        InitialStatus = "UNKNOWN"
        AttemptsMade  = 0
        FinalStatus   = "UNKNOWN"
        Escalated     = $false
        CheckedAt     = Get-Date -Format "yyyy-MM-dd HH:mm:ss"
    }

    $Svc = Get-Service -ComputerName $ServerName -Name $ServiceName -ErrorAction SilentlyContinue
    if ($null -eq $Svc) {
        Write-Log "$ServiceName not found on $ServerName" "ERROR"
        $Result.FinalStatus = "NOT FOUND"
        return $Result
    }
    $Result.InitialStatus = $Svc.Status

    if ($Svc.Status -eq "Running") {                     # Nothing to do — service is already healthy
        $Result.FinalStatus = "ALREADY RUNNING"
        return $Result
    }

    Write-Log "$ServiceName on $ServerName is $($Svc.Status). Beginning recovery ($Criticality)." "WARNING"

    $Attempt = 0
    do {                                                     # do-until: run at least once, then repeat until recovered or capped
        $Attempt++
        Start-Sleep -Seconds $BackoffSeconds[$Attempt-1]      # Increasing wait before each attempt (5s, 10s, 15s)
        Write-Log "Attempt $Attempt of $MaxAttempts : restarting $ServiceName on $ServerName" "INFO"

        try {
            Get-Service -ComputerName $ServerName -Name $ServiceName | Start-Service -ErrorAction Stop   # The actual recovery action
        }
        catch {
            Write-Log "Attempt $Attempt failed to start $ServiceName : $($_.Exception.Message)" "ERROR"
        }

        $CurrentState = (Get-Service -ComputerName $ServerName -Name $ServiceName).Status   # Re-check after the attempt

    } until ($CurrentState -eq "Running" -or $Attempt -ge $MaxAttempts)   # Stop when recovered OR out of attempts — whichever comes first

    $Result.AttemptsMade = $Attempt
    $Result.FinalStatus  = $CurrentState

    if ($CurrentState -eq "Running") {
        Write-Log "$ServiceName on $ServerName RECOVERED after $Attempt attempt(s)" "INFO"
    }
    else {
        Write-Log "$ServiceName on $ServerName FAILED to recover after $MaxAttempts attempts" "ERROR"
        if ($Criticality -eq "Critical") {                   # Only page a human for Critical services, per Priya's rule
            Send-EscalationAlert -ServerName $ServerName -ServiceName $ServiceName -AttemptsMade $Attempt
            $Result.Escalated = $true
        }
    }

    return $Result
}

# ============================================================
# SECTION 4: MAIN EXECUTION
# ============================================================

Write-Log "===== Starting Service Recovery Sweep for $($ServiceList.Count) service(s) =====" "INFO"

$AllResults = foreach ($Entry in $ServiceList) {
    Restore-Service -ServerName $Entry.ServerName -ServiceName $Entry.ServiceName -Criticality $Entry.Criticality
}

$AllResults | Sort-Object FinalStatus | Format-Table -AutoSize

$AllResults | Export-Csv -Path $ReportCSV -NoTypeInformation

Write-Log "===== Service Recovery Sweep Completed. Report: $ReportCSV =====" "INFO"

07 Line-by-Line Explanation

[ValidateSet("Critical","Routine")][string]$Criticality
PowerShell checks this constraint automatically before the function body even runs — passing anything other than exactly "Critical" or "Routine" produces an immediate, clear error instead of silently misbehaving later in the escalation logic.
do { ... } until ($CurrentState -eq "Running" -or $Attempt -ge $MaxAttempts)
The loop body always runs at least once (unlike a while loop, which checks the condition first). It keeps repeating until either the service is confirmed running or we've hit the attempt cap — whichever happens first, guaranteed to stop.
Start-Sleep -Seconds $BackoffSeconds[$Attempt-1]
Array indexing is zero-based, so attempt 1 uses index 0 (5 seconds), attempt 2 uses index 1 (10 seconds), and attempt 3 uses index 2 (15 seconds) — a simple, readable way to implement increasing backoff without a formula.
if ($Criticality -eq "Critical") { Send-EscalationAlert ... }
This is the line that directly implements Priya's rule from the story: routine services that fail to recover are logged quietly for the morning report; critical ones page a human immediately.
Common mistake: wrapping the escalation email itself in the same try/catch as the recovery logic. If sending the alert fails too (SMTP relay down), you want that failure logged clearly and separately — silently losing your one and only escalation path is the worst possible failure mode here.

08 Script Execution Flow

StepWhat Happens
1. Initial State CheckIf the service is already Running, the script exits early for that entry — no unnecessary action taken on a healthy service.
2. Retry Loop BeginsThe do-until loop waits (backoff), attempts a restart, then re-checks state — every iteration produces its own log line.
3. Loop ExitExits either because the service reports Running, or because the attempt counter reached the cap — never runs forever.
4. Branch: SuccessRecovery logged as INFO; no alert sent — this is the quiet, "nobody needs to wake up" path.
5. Branch: FailureLogged as ERROR; if the service is Critical, Send-EscalationAlert fires an actual email through the SMTP relay.
6. ReportingEvery service's full attempt history and outcome lands in the CSV report, same pattern as every prior project.

09 Windows Internals — In Simple English

How Start-Service Talks to the SCM

Start-Service sends a start request to the Service Control Manager, the same subsystem Get-Service reads from — it's the OS component actually responsible for launching and tracking every Windows service.

Windows' Own Built-In Recovery Options

Windows itself has a native "Recovery" tab per service (viewable via sc.exe qfailure) that can auto-restart a crashed service without any script at all — our PowerShell approach adds the escalation and cross-service reporting Windows' built-in feature doesn't provide.

How SMTP Relays Work

An internal SMTP relay accepts mail from trusted internal systems (like this automation host) and forwards it onward, without requiring each sender to authenticate with a personal mailbox password.

12 Production Best Practices

Always cap retries — an unbounded retry loop against a genuinely broken service just delays the human response that was actually needed.
Always apply backoff between attempts — instant repeated retries can hammer the same transient failure without giving it a chance to clear.
Separate "log it" from "page a human" by criticality — alert fatigue from paging on every minor blip erodes trust in the alerts that matter.
Never let your escalation path silently fail — log alert-sending failures distinctly, since that's your last line of defense.

13 Interview Preparation

Q: Why cap retries instead of letting the script keep trying indefinitely?
A service that won't recover after several reasonable attempts likely has a genuine root cause — continuing to retry indefinitely wastes time and can mask a problem that actually needs human investigation, instead of surfacing it quickly.
Q: Why use backoff (increasing wait time) instead of retrying instantly?
An instant retry can hit the exact same transient condition that caused the first failure; a short, increasing pause gives things like resource contention or a dependency service time to recover before the next attempt.
Q: How did you decide which services get email escalation and which don't?
By business criticality, driven by config, not code — services tied to core banking functions escalate to a human on failure; lower-impact routine services are simply logged for the next scheduled review.
Q: What would you add before trusting this fully in production?
A way to temporarily disable auto-recovery for a specific service during planned maintenance, so the automation doesn't fight against an intentional stop.

14 Key Learnings & Assignment

Key Learnings

  • Self-healing automation needs a hard retry cap and backoff, never unbounded retries
  • Do-until loops are the natural fit for "keep trying until success or limit reached"
  • Escalation should be driven by business criticality, configured externally, not hardcoded
  • The alert path itself needs its own error handling — it's your last line of defense
