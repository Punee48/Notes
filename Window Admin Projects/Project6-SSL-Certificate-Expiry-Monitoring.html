Project 06 — SSL Certificate Expiry Monitoring

The outage nobody codes for, until it happens to them: a certificate simply expires

No crash, no stopped service, no failed patch — just a calendar date quietly arriving. Today you'll check certificates two ways: what's actually installed in the local certificate store, and what a live TLS handshake reports for a public-facing banking URL, then flag anything expiring soon before it becomes an outage.

01 The Production Story

A different flavor of incident this time — nothing crashed, nothing was patched wrong. Something just... expired.
Friday, 6:00 AM — Industry War Story, Retold at Standup

Priya opens the morning standup with a story instead of a status update. "A few years ago, at my last bank, our partner integration gateway just stopped working one Saturday morning. No error in the logs pointed anywhere useful at first. Support escalated for two hours before someone finally noticed: the SSL certificate on that endpoint had expired at midnight. Nobody patched anything, nobody broke anything — a date on a calendar simply arrived, and every partner connection started failing TLS validation instantly."

"The infuriating part," she continues, "is that certificate expiry is completely predictable. We know the exact date months in advance. There's no excuse for it to ever cause an outage — except that nobody was watching the calendar."

"So today's project: check every certificate we care about — both what's sitting in the Windows certificate store on our servers, and what a real TLS connection to our public banking URLs actually presents to a client — and tell us how many days are left. I want three zones: plenty of time, getting close, and act now."

This project reinforces something important: not every production risk is a crash or a bug. A perfectly working system can still fail the instant a completely predictable date arrives — which is exactly why proactive monitoring beats reactive firefighting.

02 Business Case

Business Requirement

Prevent outages caused by expired SSL/TLS certificates on customer and partner-facing banking endpoints.

Customer Requirement

Ops wants advance warning — weeks, not hours — before any certificate expires, with clear urgency tiers.

Banking Scenario

Online banking, partner integrations, and internal service-to-service calls all rely on valid TLS certificates to establish trust and encryption.

Current Manual Process

Certificates are tracked, if at all, in a spreadsheet someone updates inconsistently whenever they remember to check.

Problems With Current Process

Entirely dependent on human memory for a completely predictable, calendar-based event — a recipe for exactly the outage Priya described.

Business Challenges

Certificates live in different places — local server stores, load balancers, and what's actually served live — and all need checking.

Risk Analysis

An expired certificate on a partner-facing endpoint causes an instant, hard failure for every connecting client — no gradual degradation, no warning.

Business Impact

A preventable, 100% predictable outage is one of the worst kinds to explain to leadership or regulators after the fact.

Why Automation Is Required

Only automation reliably checks every certificate, every day, without depending on someone remembering a spreadsheet.

Solution Design

A PowerShell script reading the local certificate store plus live TLS handshakes against key URLs, tiered by days-until-expiry.

Project Scope

Phase 1 covers local machine store certificates and a defined list of public-facing URLs, with a three-tier expiry warning system.

Expected Benefits

Certificate renewals become a scheduled, calm task weeks in advance instead of a frantic Saturday morning incident.

03 New Concepts (Building on Projects 01–05)

You already know remoting, config files, and retry logic. Here's what's new for working with certificates and dates.
The Cert: PSDrive

What it is: Just like IIS:\ in Project 04, Windows exposes installed certificates as a navigable drive: Cert:\LocalMachine\My is the standard location for server certificates bound to services like IIS.

Why it's used today: Get-ChildItem Cert:\LocalMachine\My returns every certificate installed on that server as real X509Certificate2 objects — no file parsing, no OpenSSL, just objects with properties.

X509Certificate2 Properties — NotAfter, Subject, Thumbprint

What it is: Every certificate object carries standard properties: .Subject (who it was issued to), .Issuer (who issued it), .NotAfter (the expiry date as a real DateTime), and .Thumbprint (a unique fingerprint identifying the exact certificate).

Why it matters: .NotAfter being a genuine DateTime object — not a text string — is what lets us do real date math on it, rather than trying to parse a date out of text.

Date Arithmetic — New-TimeSpan & Subtracting Dates

What it is: PowerShell dates are real .NET DateTime objects, so you can simply subtract one from another: ($Cert.NotAfter - (Get-Date)).Days gives you the exact number of days remaining. New-TimeSpan is a cmdlet that does the same subtraction more explicitly.

Why it's used today: This single calculation is the heart of the entire project — everything else exists to feed a certificate's expiry date into this subtraction.

Checking a LIVE Certificate via .NET — SslStream

What it is: The local certificate store only tells you what's installed — it doesn't prove that's actually what a real client sees over the network (a load balancer might be serving a different one). To check the certificate a real TLS connection presents, we drop down to raw .NET classes: System.Net.Sockets.TcpClient to open the connection, and System.Net.Security.SslStream to perform the TLS handshake and read back the presented certificate.

Why it's used today: This is the same technique tools like openssl s_client use — connecting exactly as a browser would and inspecting what's actually served live, which is the only way to be sure what customers really experience.

Production nuance: PowerShell can call almost any .NET class directly, not just its own cmdlets — this is a good first taste of that much larger capability.

Calculated Properties in Select-Object

What it is: Select-Object can create a brand new column on the fly using a hash table: @{Name="DaysLeft"; Expression={($_.NotAfter - (Get-Date)).Days}} — instead of adding a whole extra step to compute and attach a value.

Why it's used today: It keeps our reporting pipeline compact — we compute "days left" as part of shaping the output, not as a separate manual loop.

Certificate expiry is the textbook example of a "boring but critical" check — nothing dynamic to detect, just simple date math done reliably, every single day, without fail. That reliability is the entire value.

04 Architecture

Two independent data sources feed the same simple date-math decision — this is deliberate: the store tells you what's installed, the live handshake proves what's actually served, and either one alone can miss a problem the other catches.

06 The Script — Test-CertificateExpiry.ps1

Scripts\Test-CertificateExpiry.ps1 
<#
.SYNOPSIS
    SSL Certificate Expiry Monitoring - ABC Bank Production Support
.DESCRIPTION
    Checks certificates in the local certificate store across a server
    list, and performs a live TLS handshake against public endpoints,
    flagging anything approaching expiry.
#>

# ============================================================
# SECTION 1: CONFIGURATION
# ============================================================

$Servers   = Get-Content -Path "C:\CertMonitor\Config\servers.txt"
$Endpoints = Get-Content -Path "C:\CertMonitor\Config\endpoints.txt"

$WarningDays  = 30                     # 30 days or fewer remaining = WARNING
$CriticalDays = 7                      # 7 days or fewer remaining  = CRITICAL

$TimeTag    = Get-Date -Format 'yyyyMMdd_HHmmss'
$LogFile    = "C:\CertMonitor\Logs\CertCheck_$TimeTag.log"
$ReportCSV  = "C:\CertMonitor\Reports\CertCheck_$TimeTag.csv"
$ReportHTML = "C:\CertMonitor\Reports\CertCheck_$TimeTag.html"

function Write-Log {
    param([string]$Message, [string]$Level = "INFO")
    $Stamp = Get-Date -Format "yyyy-MM-dd HH:mm:ss"
    $Entry = "[$Stamp] [$Level] $Message"
    Add-Content -Path $LogFile -Value $Entry
    Write-Host $Entry
}

function Get-ExpiryVerdict {                       # Shared tiering logic used by BOTH check types below
    param([int]$DaysLeft)
    if     ($DaysLeft -le $CriticalDays) { "CRITICAL" }
    elseif ($DaysLeft -le $WarningDays)  { "WARNING" }
    else                                    { "OK" }
}

# ============================================================
# SECTION 2: LOCAL CERTIFICATE STORE CHECK
# ============================================================

function Get-StoreCertificates {
    param([string]$ComputerName)

    try {
        $Certs = Invoke-Command -ComputerName $ComputerName -ScriptBlock {
            Get-ChildItem -Path "Cert:\LocalMachine\My" |             # Every certificate bound to services on this server
                Where-Object { $_.NotAfter -gt (Get-Date).AddYears(-5) }  # Skip long-expired junk certs cluttering the store
        }

        foreach ($Cert in $Certs) {
            $DaysLeft = ($Cert.NotAfter - (Get-Date)).Days              # Real DateTime subtraction — gives whole days remaining
            [PSCustomObject]@{
                Source      = "LocalStore"
                ServerOrUrl = $ComputerName
                Subject     = $Cert.Subject
                Issuer      = $Cert.Issuer
                Thumbprint  = $Cert.Thumbprint
                ExpiryDate  = $Cert.NotAfter
                DaysLeft    = $DaysLeft
                Verdict     = Get-ExpiryVerdict -DaysLeft $DaysLeft
            }
        }
    }
    catch {
        Write-Log "Error reading certificate store on $ComputerName : $($_.Exception.Message)" "ERROR"
    }
}

# ============================================================
# SECTION 3: LIVE TLS HANDSHAKE CHECK
# ============================================================

function Get-LiveCertificate {
    param([string]$HostName, [int]$Port = 443)

    try {
        $TcpClient = New-Object System.Net.Sockets.TcpClient($HostName, $Port)   # Opens a raw network connection, like a browser would
        $SslStream = New-Object System.Net.Security.SslStream($TcpClient.GetStream(), $false, ({ $true }))  # Wraps it in TLS; callback accepts the cert so we can INSPECT it even if untrusted
        $SslStream.AuthenticateAsClient($HostName)              # Performs the actual TLS handshake against the real endpoint

        $RemoteCert = [System.Security.Cryptography.X509Certificates.X509Certificate2]$SslStream.RemoteCertificate  # The certificate the server ACTUALLY presented
        $DaysLeft = ($RemoteCert.NotAfter - (Get-Date)).Days

        [PSCustomObject]@{
            Source      = "LiveTLS"
            ServerOrUrl = $HostName
            Subject     = $RemoteCert.Subject
            Issuer      = $RemoteCert.Issuer
            Thumbprint  = $RemoteCert.Thumbprint
            ExpiryDate  = $RemoteCert.NotAfter
            DaysLeft    = $DaysLeft
            Verdict     = Get-ExpiryVerdict -DaysLeft $DaysLeft
        }

        $SslStream.Close(); $TcpClient.Close()             # Always clean up network resources when done
    }
    catch {
        Write-Log "Error connecting to $HostName : $($_.Exception.Message)" "ERROR"
        [PSCustomObject]@{
            Source = "LiveTLS"; ServerOrUrl = $HostName; Subject = "N/A"; Issuer = "N/A"
            Thumbprint = "N/A"; ExpiryDate = $null; DaysLeft = $null; Verdict = "CRITICAL - CONNECTION FAILED"
        }
    }
}

# ============================================================
# SECTION 4: MAIN EXECUTION
# ============================================================

Write-Log "===== Starting Certificate Expiry Check =====" "INFO"

$StoreResults = foreach ($Server in $Servers)   { Get-StoreCertificates -ComputerName $Server }
$LiveResults  = foreach ($Endpoint in $Endpoints) { Get-LiveCertificate -HostName $Endpoint }

$AllResults = $StoreResults + $LiveResults          # Combine both sources into one report

$AllResults |
    Select-Object Source, ServerOrUrl, Subject, DaysLeft, Verdict,
        @{ Name="ExpiryDate"; Expression = { $_.ExpiryDate.ToString("yyyy-MM-dd") } } |  # Calculated property: format the date cleanly for the report
    Sort-Object DaysLeft |                                                       # Most urgent (soonest expiry) appears first
    Format-Table -AutoSize

$AllResults | Export-Csv -Path $ReportCSV -NoTypeInformation
$AllResults | ConvertTo-Html -Title "ABC Bank Certificate Expiry Report" | Out-File -FilePath $ReportHTML

$CriticalCount = ($AllResults | Where-Object { $_.Verdict -like "CRITICAL*" }).Count
if ($CriticalCount -gt 0) {
    Write-Log "$CriticalCount certificate(s) are CRITICAL - expiring within $CriticalDays days or connection failed" "WARNING"
}

Write-Log "===== Certificate Expiry Check Completed. Report: $ReportHTML =====" "INFO"

07 Line-by-Line Explanation

($Cert.NotAfter - (Get-Date)).Days
Because .NotAfter is a real DateTime object, subtracting Get-Date (right now) from it produces a TimeSpan object automatically — .Days pulls out just the whole-day count from that span. No string parsing, no manual date math formulas.
New-Object System.Net.Security.SslStream($TcpClient.GetStream(), $false, ({ $true }))
The third argument is a certificate validation callback — normally .NET would reject an untrusted or self-signed certificate outright. Passing a callback that always returns $true tells .NET "let me inspect this certificate regardless of trust status," which is exactly what a monitoring tool needs, even though a real browser would rightly block it.
[System.Security.Cryptography.X509Certificates.X509Certificate2]$SslStream.RemoteCertificate
The stream returns the certificate as a more generic type; this explicit cast converts it into the richer X509Certificate2 type so we can read .NotAfter, .Subject, and .Thumbprint directly.
@{ Name="ExpiryDate"; Expression = { $_.ExpiryDate.ToString("yyyy-MM-dd") } }
A calculated property inside Select-Object: instead of exporting the full DateTime object (with time, timezone, etc.), this reformats it into a clean, sortable date string just for the report — the underlying data isn't changed, only how it's displayed here.
Common mistake: forgetting to close the TcpClient/SslStream after use. Each check opens a real network socket — leaving many of them open during a large sweep can exhaust available connections on the automation host over time.

08 Script Execution Flow

StepWhat Happens
1. Store CheckFor each server, Invoke-Command reads that server's own local certificate store — no network handshake involved, just reading configuration.
2. Live CheckFor each public endpoint, a real TCP connection and TLS handshake happens from the automation host, exactly like a customer's browser would perform.
3. Shared Verdict LogicBoth check types funnel through the same Get-ExpiryVerdict function — one tiering rule, applied consistently regardless of source.
4. Combine & SortStore and live results are combined into one array and sorted by DaysLeft, so the most urgent certificate — regardless of source — appears first.
5. ReportingSame CSV/HTML/log pattern as every prior project — consistency across the whole automation suite, not a one-off format.

09 Windows Internals — In Simple English

Where Windows Stores Certificates

The Local Machine certificate store is a Windows-managed database of certificates and their private keys, organized into named "stores" like My (personal), Root (trusted authorities), and CA (intermediate authorities).

How a TLS Handshake Actually Works

Before any encrypted data flows, the client and server exchange certificates and cryptographic parameters in a handshake — the server's certificate (including its expiry date) is presented during this very first step, before anything else happens.

Why Expired Certificates Fail Instantly

TLS clients check the certificate's validity dates as a hard rule, not a soft warning — the moment NotAfter passes, standards-compliant clients refuse the connection outright, with no gradual degradation.

12 Production Best Practices

Check the live, actually-served certificate, not just what's installed locally — a load balancer or reverse proxy can serve a different certificate than what a server's local store shows.
Always close TCP/SSL connections explicitly after each check — a monitoring script that leaks connections is itself a small production risk.
Give yourself weeks of warning, not days — certificate renewal often involves a third-party Certificate Authority and approval steps that take real time.
Sort urgency-based reports by the most time-critical item first — nobody should have to scan 500 rows to find the certificate expiring tomorrow.

13 Interview Preparation

Q: Why check both the local certificate store AND a live TLS handshake?
The local store shows what's installed on that specific server, but load balancers, reverse proxies, or CDN layers in front of it can present a completely different certificate to real clients — only the live check confirms what customers actually receive.
Q: Why does the SslStream validation callback always return true here?
A monitoring tool needs to inspect a certificate's details regardless of whether it's currently trusted or already expired — returning true from the callback lets the handshake complete so we can read the certificate, instead of .NET blocking the connection the way a real browser correctly would.
Q: How would you make this a fully proactive, zero-surprise process?
Tie the CRITICAL tier into the same escalation email pattern from Project 05, and set the WARNING threshold far enough out (30+ days) to comfortably cover the real-world lead time a Certificate Authority renewal typically requires.
Q: What's a real incident this kind of check prevents?
Exactly the story that opened this project — a partner integration or customer-facing endpoint failing instantly and completely the moment a completely predictable expiry date arrives, with zero advance warning to the team.
