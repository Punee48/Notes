Deployment Validation — Automatic Health-Check Gates and Change Closure

Project 1 got the Change Request created and approved automatically. Now we close the other end of the loop: proving the deployment actually worked, closing the Change Request without a human, and automatically kicking off rollback when it didn't.

01 The Production Story — ABC Bank, Two Weeks Later

Knowledge Transfer Session — Principal Architect to DevOps Engineer

The automated Change creation from Project 1 has been live for two weeks. Time-to-CR dropped from 45 minutes to under 90 seconds, and the audit team is happy — every CR now has a build reference.

But a new problem showed up in the weekly release review. Change Requests are being created and approved automatically, deployments are running, but nobody is closing the CRs afterward. Coordinators are back to manually checking Jenkins logs, manually checking if the app is actually healthy in production, and manually setting the CR to "Closed Complete" — sometimes a day later, sometimes never. Compliance flagged 34 Change Requests last month sitting in "Implement" state for over 48 hours with no closure.

Worse, two weeks ago a bad deployment to Internet Banking passed the gate, went to production, immediately started throwing errors, and nobody rolled it back for 22 minutes because there was no automatic signal that the deployment had actually failed post-release.

Our manager's next milestone: after every deployment, ServiceNow must automatically verify the application is healthy, close the Change Request if it is, and automatically trigger a rollback job in Jenkins if it isn't — with no manual log-checking.

02 Business & Customer Requirement

Business Requirement

Every Change Request must reach a final state (Closed Complete or Closed Unsuccessful) within minutes of the deployment finishing, backed by an actual health signal from the application — not a human's best guess.

Customer Requirement (Head of Production Support)

Reduce mean-time-to-rollback for a bad deployment from 20+ minutes to under 3 minutes by making rollback an automatic reaction to a failed health check, not something a human has to notice and decide.

03 Current State, Problems, Risks

Current process (after Project 1)

  1. Jenkins deploy stage finishes — pipeline shows green in Jenkins, but ServiceNow has no idea the deploy stage even ran.
  2. Coordinator manually checks the app's health dashboard (Grafana) some time later.
  3. If healthy, coordinator manually sets the CR to Closed Complete.
  4. If unhealthy, coordinator manually pings the on-call engineer, who manually re-runs a rollback Jenkins job.

Operational Risk

Every minute a bad deployment stays live on Internet Banking is customer-facing transaction risk — failed logins, failed fund transfers.

Compliance Risk

CRs stuck open past their planned end date are a direct audit finding — the bank's Change Management policy requires closure within 4 business hours of implementation.

Business impact

34 unclosed CRs in a single month, one production incident where an unhealthy deployment stayed live for 22 minutes before manual rollback — both are now documented in the last Production Support postmortem.

04 Why Health-Check-Driven Closure (and why not alternatives)

Why this approach: we make ServiceNow the decision-maker again, exactly like Project 1's gate pattern — Jenkins tells ServiceNow "deployment finished," ServiceNow independently verifies health by calling the application's own health endpoint, and ServiceNow decides close-vs-rollback. This keeps one consistent governance pattern across the whole DevOps Change Velocity design instead of inventing a new one.

AlternativeWhy it was rejected
Trust Jenkins' own "deploy succeeded" signal as sufficientA deploy script can exit 0 while the application is still crash-looping — "deployed" and "healthy" are not the same thing.
Manual closure with a reminder notificationThis is what we already have (informally) and it's exactly what's failing — reminders get ignored under workload.
Have the monitoring tool (Grafana) call ServiceNow directlyWould require a second, separate integration and duplicate correlation logic; better to have Jenkins orchestrate the single flow that already carries the correlation_id.
Architect's note: Notice the design principle repeating from Project 1 — never let the tool that performs the action also certify that the action was safe. Jenkins deploys; it does not get to decide the deployment was healthy. That's ServiceNow's job, based on independent evidence.

05 Core Concepts

Deployment Validation — what it is, why it exists, how it works internally

What it is: An automatic post-deployment check that calls the application's real health endpoint (or a synthetic transaction) and feeds the result back into the Change Request lifecycle.

Why it exists: Change Management's real job isn't "did we get permission to deploy," it's "did the change actually succeed safely." Without validation, Change Management stops at the gate and has no visibility into what happened after.

Internal working: Jenkins calls a new endpoint after the deploy stage, which stores a "post_deploy" orchestration task. A Flow reacts to that insert, calls an outbound REST message to the application's /health endpoint using RESTMessageV2, and based on the HTTP status and response body, either closes the CR or flags it and calls a rollback job.

Real banking example: Internet Banking exposes /actuator/health (Spring Boot health endpoint). ServiceNow calls it right after deploy; a 200 with {"status":"UP"} closes the change; anything else triggers rollback.

Alternative approaches: Passive monitoring integration (Grafana/Datadog webhook into ServiceNow) is powerful for ongoing incident detection, but for the specific "did THIS deployment succeed" question, an active, synchronous health check right after deploy is faster and more precise.

Best practice: Always add a short "settle time" (e.g., wait 30 seconds) between deploy finishing and the first health check — checking a service that hasn't finished starting up yet produces false negatives.

Common mistake: Checking HTTP status only (200 vs non-200) and ignoring the response body. Many health endpoints return 200 with a body saying a dependency is down — you must parse the body, not just the status code.

Rollback Automation — what it is and why it must be a Change-driven trigger, not a human decision

What it is: An automatic call from ServiceNow back into Jenkins to run a pre-defined rollback job for that specific application, fired the moment a health check fails.

Why it exists: Every minute a bad deployment stays live is customer risk. Removing the "human notices, human decides, human triggers" chain removes the biggest source of delay.

Real banking example: Health check for Internet Banking returns DOWN → ServiceNow calls Jenkins' internet-banking-rollback job via its remote build trigger API, and simultaneously reopens the Change Request work notes with the failure reason for the postmortem.

DORA Metrics — what they are and why this project feeds them

What it is: Four industry-standard metrics — Deployment Frequency, Lead Time for Changes, Change Failure Rate, and Mean Time to Restore — that DevOps Change Velocity can calculate automatically once orchestration tasks and Change Requests are properly correlated and closed.

Why it matters here: Change Failure Rate and MTTR are both directly computed from the data this project produces — a Change Request that never closes, or closes without recording whether it succeeded, breaks both metrics. This project is what makes those metrics trustworthy.

07 Complete Solution Design

  • New fields on change_request: u_health_check_url (where to validate) and u_last_validation_result (audit trail of the last check).
  • Inbound endpoint: POST /api/x_bank/devops/deployment_result — Jenkins calls this right after the deploy stage, before declaring the pipeline finished.
  • Script Include: x_bank_devops.DeploymentValidator — wraps the outbound health check call and the pass/fail decision logic, reusable across every app's Flow.
  • Flow Designer flow: "Validate Deployment & Close Change" — triggered on the new post_deploy orchestration task, waits 30 seconds, calls the validator, branches to close or flag+rollback.
  • Business Rule: on Change Request update, when u_last_validation_result changes to "failed," calls the rollback trigger.
  • Reconciliation job: a Scheduled Job as a safety net — finds any CR still open more than 2 hours after its planned end date and pages the on-call engineer, in case the Flow itself ever fails silently.

09 Implementation Step 2 — DeploymentValidator Script Include

Script Include · x_bank_devops.DeploymentValidator
var DeploymentValidator = Class.create();                        // Standard ServiceNow class pattern for a reusable server-side object
DeploymentValidator.prototype = {

    initialize: function() {},                                     // No setup needed here, but the constructor must still exist

    // Entry point: takes a Change Request sys_id, returns { passed: true/false, detail: string }
    validate: function(changeSysId) {
        var cr = new GlideRecord('change_request');                 // Opens a GlideRecord cursor on the Change Request table
        if (!cr.get(changeSysId)) {                                  // Loads the specific CR; false means it doesn't exist
            return { passed: false, detail: 'Change Request not found: ' + changeSysId }; // Fail closed — never assume success when we can't even find the record
        }

        var healthUrl = cr.getValue('u_health_check_url');           // Reads the health endpoint URL stored on the CR
        if (!healthUrl) {                                             // Guard clause — some apps may not have a health URL configured yet
            return { passed: false, detail: 'No health check URL configured for this change' };
        }

        var result = this._callHealthEndpoint(healthUrl);            // Delegates the actual HTTP call to a private helper (kept separate so it's independently testable)
        return result;
    },

    _callHealthEndpoint: function(url) {
        try {
            var request = new sn_ws.RESTMessageV2();                 // Creates a scripted outbound REST message object
            request.setHttpMethod('GET');                             // Health checks are always simple GET calls
            request.setEndpoint(url);                                 // Sets the target URL — the app's own health endpoint, not a ServiceNow URL
            request.setRequestHeader('Accept', 'application/json');   // Tells the app we expect a JSON response body

            var response = request.execute();                         // Sends the HTTP request synchronously and waits for the response
            var httpStatus = response.getStatusCode();                 // Reads the numeric HTTP status code returned by the app
            var body = response.getBody();                             // Reads the raw response body as a string

            if (httpStatus != 200) {                                    // Any non-200 status is treated as unhealthy, regardless of body content
                return { passed: false, detail: 'Health endpoint returned HTTP ' + httpStatus };
            }

            var parsed = JSON.parse(body);                              // Parses the JSON response body into a usable object
            if (parsed.status == 'UP') {                                 // Checks the actual application-reported status, not just the HTTP code
                return { passed: true, detail: 'Health check passed: status=UP' };
            }

            return { passed: false, detail: 'Health endpoint reported status=' + parsed.status }; // Covers the "200 OK but app says DOWN/DEGRADED" case explicitly

        } catch (ex) {                                                  // Catches network failures, timeouts, or malformed JSON
            return { passed: false, detail: 'Health check exception: ' + ex.getMessage() }; // Fail closed again — an exception is treated as a failed validation, never a silent pass
        }
    },

    type: 'DeploymentValidator'                                        // Required — must match the class name exactly
};

Line-by-line: what matters most here

sn_ws.RESTMessageV2

This is ServiceNow's scripted outbound HTTP client. It works with any REST endpoint — internal or external — and every call runs server-side, so no CORS restrictions apply the way they would in browser JavaScript.

httpStatus != 200 check first

We check the transport-layer status before ever trying to parse the body. If the body isn't valid JSON (e.g., an HTML error page from a proxy timeout), attempting to parse it first would throw before we even know it failed — always validate the cheap thing first.

try / catch around the whole call

Outbound HTTP calls can fail in ways that have nothing to do with the app being unhealthy — DNS failure, TLS handshake failure, timeout. All of these must still result in a controlled "failed" response, never an uncaught script exception that would abort the whole Flow.

Production best practice: "Fail closed" is the theme of this whole class — every code path that can't positively confirm health returns passed: false. In banking, a deployment validator that fails open (assumes healthy when uncertain) is far more dangerous than one that occasionally over-triggers a rollback.
Common mistake: Forgetting a timeout on the outbound REST message. If the app is genuinely hung (not just returning an error, but not responding at all), an unbounded call can leave the Flow — and the whole validation step — stuck indefinitely. Set an explicit timeout via request.setHttpTimeout(15000) in production.

10 Implementation Step 3 — Scripted REST: Deployment Result

Scripted REST Resource · POST /api/x_bank/devops/deployment_result
(function process(/*RESTAPIRequest*/ request, /*RESTAPIResponse*/ response) {

    var body = request.body.data;                                     // Parses the incoming JSON POST body into a usable object automatically

    if (!body.correlation_id) {                                        // Guard clause — we cannot proceed without knowing which build/change this belongs to
        response.setStatus(400);
        response.setBody({ error: 'correlation_id is required' });
        return;
    }

    var task = new GlideRecord('sn_devops_orchestration_task');       // Opens a GlideRecord to write a new orchestration event
    task.initialize();                                                  // Prepares an empty new record in memory
    task.correlation_id = body.correlation_id;                          // Same correlation_id used in Project 1 — this is what ties everything together
    task.pipeline_name = body.pipeline_name;                            // Records which application pipeline this event belongs to
    task.stage_name = 'post_deploy';                                    // This literal string is what our Flow's trigger condition filters on
    task.build_url = body.build_url;                                    // Kept for traceability, same as the original build event
    var taskId = task.insert();                                         // Saves the record and returns its new sys_id — this insert is what fires the Flow

    response.setStatus(202);                                            // HTTP 202 = Accepted: we've recorded the event, but validation happens asynchronously in the Flow
    response.setBody({ orchestration_task: taskId, status: 'validation_queued' }); // Tells Jenkins we received it; Jenkins does not need to wait synchronously for this call

})(request, response);
HTTP 202 vs 200

202 Accepted specifically communicates "we got your request, processing will happen asynchronously" — more accurate than 200 here, since the actual health check and CR closure happen later inside the Flow, not inside this REST call.

Design note: This endpoint deliberately does almost nothing except record the event and let the Flow do the real work. Keeping Scripted REST resources thin and pushing logic into Flows/Script Includes keeps the integration layer easy to reason about and easy to unit test.

11 Implementation Step 4 — Flow: "Validate Deployment & Close Change"

Flow settingValue
Trigger typeRecord Created on sn_devops_orchestration_task
Conditionstage_name is post_deploy

Flow steps

  1. Look Up Records — find the Change Request where correlation_id matches the trigger record. If none found, End Flow (nothing to validate).
  2. Wait — pause 30 seconds, giving the application time to finish starting up after deploy (this is the "settle time" mentioned in Section 5).
  3. Script Step — call DeploymentValidator.validate() with the CR's sys_id, store the result object in flow variables passed and detail.
  4. Update Record — set u_last_validation_result to "passed" or "failed" based on the script output, and append detail to work notes.
  5. Flow Logic → If — passed is true.
    • True branch: Update Record — set state = "Closed Complete," close_code = "successful."
    • False branch: nothing further here — the Business Rule in Step 5 reacts to the u_last_validation_result field change and handles rollback, keeping the Flow focused only on validation, not remediation.
Production best practice: Notice the Flow does NOT trigger the rollback directly — that's delegated to a Business Rule reacting to the field change. This separation means any future automation (not just this Flow) that sets u_last_validation_result = failed also gets rollback behavior for free, without duplicating logic.

12 Implementation Step 5 — Business Rule: Trigger Rollback on Failed Validation

Business Rule · Trigger Rollback (after, update, condition: u_last_validation_result changes to 'failed')
(function executeRule(current, previous) {

    if (current.u_last_validation_result != 'failed') {              // Defense in depth — even though the Business Rule condition already filters this, we re-check in script
        return;
    }

    var rollbackJob = current.getValue('u_rollback_job_name');        // Reads which Jenkins job to call, stored per-application on the CR
    if (!rollbackJob) {
        current.work_notes = 'Validation failed but no rollback job configured — manual intervention required.'; // If we don't know how to roll back automatically, we say so loudly instead of doing nothing silently
        current.update();
        return;
    }

    var request = new sn_ws.RESTMessageV2();                          // Creates an outbound REST call, this time TO Jenkins instead of to the application
    request.setHttpMethod('POST');
    request.setEndpoint('https://jenkins.abcbank.internal/job/' + rollbackJob + '/build'); // Jenkins' remote build trigger URL pattern for the specific rollback job
    request.setRequestHeader('Authorization', 'Bearer ' + gs.getProperty('x_bank_devops.jenkins_token')); // Reads the integration token from a protected System Property, never hardcoded in script
    var response = request.execute();                                  // Fires the rollback build

    current.work_notes = 'Automatic rollback triggered: job=' + rollbackJob + ', Jenkins response=' + response.getStatusCode(); // Records exactly what automated action was taken, for the postmortem
    current.update();                                                    // Saves the work note — this is an "after" rule, so we must call update() explicitly

})(current, previous);
gs.getProperty(...)

Reads a System Property rather than hardcoding the Jenkins token in script — this is the standard ServiceNow pattern for secrets, and it means rotating the token never requires a code change or deployment.

after, update + current.update()

Unlike the "before" rule in Project 1, this one deliberately runs "after" because it performs an external side effect (calling Jenkins) — you never want an external HTTP call inside a "before" rule, since a database rollback wouldn't undo the external call.

Common mistake: Putting the outbound rollback call in the same Flow that runs validation. If the Flow errors out partway (e.g., a platform hiccup) between "mark failed" and "trigger rollback," you can end up with a CR marked failed and no rollback ever fired, with no separate mechanism to catch it. Splitting it into a Business Rule reacting to the field change means the reconciliation job in Step 7 can also catch missed rollbacks.

14 Implementation Step 7 — Reconciliation Scheduled Job (Safety Net)

Scheduled Job · Reconcile Stuck Change Requests (runs every 30 minutes)
(function() {
    var gr = new GlideRecord('change_request');                        // Opens a GlideRecord on the Change Request table
    gr.addQuery('state', 'IN', '2,3');                                  // Filters to CRs still in "Scheduled" or "Implement" states — not yet closed
    gr.addQuery('u_last_validation_result', 'not_run');                 // Only CRs where our Flow never even completed validation
    var oneHourAgo = new GlideDateTime();
    oneHourAgo.addSeconds(-3600);                                       // Computes a timestamp exactly one hour before right now
    gr.addQuery('sys_created_on', '<', oneHourAgo);                     // Only CRs created more than an hour ago — gives normal processing time to complete first
    gr.query();                                                          // Executes the query

    while (gr.next()) {                                                  // Loops through every matching stuck CR
        gs.eventQueue('x_bank.change_stuck_alert', gr, gr.getValue('number')); // Fires a custom ServiceNow event, which a separate Notification is configured to send to the on-call Slack/Teams channel
    }
})();
gs.eventQueue(...)

Rather than sending a notification directly from this script, we raise an event and let ServiceNow's native Notification engine handle delivery — this keeps the alerting channel (Slack, Teams, email) configurable without ever touching this script again.

Production best practice: This job exists purely as a safety net in case the Flow itself fails to run (platform outage, a bug, a malformed payload). Always build one of these for any automation with real operational consequences — automation that silently fails is worse than no automation at all.

15 Execution Flow — What Actually Happens, Step by Step

  1. Jenkins finishes the "Deploy to Production" stage (unchanged from Project 1).
  2. "Report Deployment Result" stage POSTs to /deployment_result with the same correlation_id used throughout the whole pipeline.
  3. The Scripted REST resource inserts a post_deploy orchestration task and returns 202 immediately.
  4. The insert fires the "Validate Deployment & Close Change" Flow.
  5. Flow finds the matching Change Request by correlation_id, waits 30 seconds for the app to settle.
  6. Flow calls DeploymentValidator.validate(), which makes a real outbound HTTP call to the app's health endpoint.
  7. Flow writes the result onto u_last_validation_result and appends work notes.
  8. If passed → Flow sets the CR to Closed Complete directly. Done — no human involved.
  9. If failed → the field change fires the "Trigger Rollback" Business Rule.
  10. Business Rule calls Jenkins' remote build API for the configured rollback job, and records exactly what it did in work notes.
  11. Every 30 minutes, the reconciliation job checks for anything that fell through — any CR still unvalidated after an hour raises an alert to on-call.

17 Local Lab — Practice This Yourself

Prerequisites

  • Everything from Project 1's lab already working (Flow, Risk Calculator, gate endpoint).
  • A simple health-check endpoint to test against — easiest is a small local Node/Express or Spring Boot app exposing GET /health returning {"status":"UP"}.
  • Postman, to simulate both success and failure health responses.

Setup steps

  1. Add the three new fields to change_request as described in Section 8.
  2. Build the DeploymentValidator Script Include exactly as shown.
  3. Build the /deployment_result Scripted REST resource.
  4. Build the Flow, using "Manually run" first with a fake orchestration task pointing at your local test health endpoint.
  5. Point u_health_check_url on a test CR at your local app's /health endpoint (use a tunnel like ngrok if your PDI can't reach localhost directly).
  6. Stop your local test app and re-run the Flow — confirm the CR gets flagged and u_last_validation_result becomes "failed."
  7. Build the rollback Business Rule and point u_rollback_job_name at a harmless test Jenkins job to confirm the remote trigger call works.

Validation checklist

  • ☐ A healthy endpoint results in the CR reaching Closed Complete with no human action.
  • ☐ An unhealthy endpoint (or a stopped app) results in u_last_validation_result = failed and a rollback call to Jenkins.
  • ☐ A CR with no u_health_check_url configured fails validation loudly instead of silently passing.
  • ☐ The reconciliation job correctly finds a CR you manually leave in "not_run" for over an hour.

19 Interview Preparation

Q: Why does the validator check both HTTP status and response body instead of just the status code?
A: Many health endpoints return HTTP 200 even when a dependency is degraded — the body carries the real application-level status. Checking status code alone would let a genuinely broken deployment pass validation.
Q: Why is the rollback trigger a separate Business Rule instead of part of the validation Flow?
A: Separation of concerns and resilience — any process that marks validation as failed (not just this one Flow) automatically gets rollback behavior, and a reconciliation job can independently catch cases where the Flow itself didn't complete.
Q: What happens if the health endpoint itself is down/unreachable, as opposed to returning an unhealthy status?
A: The RESTMessageV2 call throws or times out, which is caught and treated as a failed validation — the design fails closed rather than assuming success on an inconclusive result.
Q: How would you prevent a flaky health check from causing unnecessary rollbacks?
A: Add a short retry (e.g., 2 attempts with a brief delay) inside the validator before declaring failure, and track false-positive rate in the Post-Implementation Review to tune settle time and retry count.
Q: Why is the reconciliation job necessary if the Flow is supposed to handle everything?
A: Any automation can fail silently — a platform outage, a bad deploy of the Flow itself, a malformed payload. The reconciliation job is an independent safety net that doesn't depend on the same code path working correctly.

20 Common Mistakes Seen in Real Implementations

  • No settle time between deploy finishing and the first health check — causes false failures on apps that take a few seconds to start serving traffic.
  • Treating "200 OK" as automatically healthy without inspecting the body.
  • Hardcoding the Jenkins rollback token directly in the Business Rule script instead of a protected System Property.
  • No reconciliation/safety-net job — when the Flow silently fails, nobody notices until an auditor does.
  • Firing the rollback call inside a "before" Business Rule, where a database transaction issue could leave the record state inconsistent with the external action already taken.
