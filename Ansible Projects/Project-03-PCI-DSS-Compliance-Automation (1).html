Project 3: PCI-DSS Compliance Automation using Red Hat AAP

SecureBank processes over ₹40,000 crore in card transactions annually, making it a Level-1 PCI-DSS merchant and service provider — the most stringent tier, requiring annual QSA (Qualified Security Assessor) audits and quarterly internal scans. This project automates the detection, remediation, and evidence collection of PCI-DSS controls across all Cardholder Data Environment (CDE) servers, replacing a 3-week manual audit preparation exercise with a continuous, automated compliance pipeline that runs every Sunday and before every QSA audit.

RHEL 8/9 Red Hat AAP 2.5 PCI-DSS v4.0 CDE Servers OpenSCAP / RHEL SCAP Auto-Remediation QSA Evidence

📋 1. Business Requirement

SecureBank's QSA audit occurs every April. The InfoSec Compliance team is responsible for proving that all 62 servers in the Cardholder Data Environment (CDE) meet PCI-DSS v4.0 controls continuously — not just during the 2-week audit prep window. Key requirements:

  • PCI-DSS Requirement 2: Apply secure configurations to all system components (CIS Benchmark Level 2 on RHEL).
  • PCI-DSS Requirement 6: Protect systems against known vulnerabilities (patch management, covered in Project 2; SCAP scanning here).
  • PCI-DSS Requirement 8: Identify users and authenticate access — enforce password policy, SSH key controls, lock idle accounts.
  • PCI-DSS Requirement 10: Log and monitor all access to system components — verify auditd, rsyslog, and log-forwarding are correctly configured.
  • Every control check must produce machine-readable evidence (HTML + XML SCAP report) that the QSA can independently verify, timestamped and signed with the AAP job ID.
  • Auto-remediation must fix low-risk findings automatically on the same run; high-risk findings (e.g., open inbound ports) must be flagged for human review but NOT auto-remediated.

🏦 2. Real-Time Banking Scenario

The Story: It's March — four weeks before SecureBank's annual QSA audit. The CISO, Meenakshi, has just been told by the compliance team that last year's audit took 18 engineer-days to manually collect screenshots, run ad-hoc commands like sshd -T | grep PermitRootLogin on each of 62 CDE servers, and paste results into a 400-row Excel checklist — and the QSA still found 7 "findings" because three servers were misconfigured post-deployment and nobody noticed. This year, Suresh, the Platform Security lead, has built a PCI-DSS compliance playbook that runs every Sunday night via an AAP scheduled job. By audit week, 52 out of 62 servers are already fully compliant; the other 10 have auto-remediation-eligible findings (SSH config, password policy, auditd rules) that the Sunday run fixes automatically. Meenakshi sends the QSA a link to the AAP Activity Stream and 62 SCAP HTML reports — the auditor is able to independently verify everything without a single manual command being run during the audit itself.

❗ 3. Problem Statement

  • PCI-DSS compliance is treated as a point-in-time audit exercise, not a continuous posture — a server misconfigured in May isn't discovered until March of the following year.
  • Manual control checks across 62 CDE servers take 3 weeks of engineer time, are inconsistently executed across different team members, and produce non-standardized evidence.
  • No distinction between auto-remediable findings (wrong sshd config value) and findings requiring human judgment (unexplained open firewall port) — everything gets lumped into a findings spreadsheet.
  • Configuration drift: a developer team deploys a new microservice and inadvertently enables password-based SSH on a CDE app server — nobody knows until the QSA finds it.
  • QSA-grade evidence requirements (signed, timestamped, independently verifiable) cannot be produced from ad-hoc command output pasted into a Word document.

🧑‍💻 5. Existing Manual Process (Before Automation)

  1. Compliance engineer SSHs into each of 62 CDE servers, one by one.
  2. Runs a checklist of ~80 commands: sshd -T | grep X, grep X /etc/login.defs, systemctl status auditd, iptables -L, etc.
  3. Copy-pastes output into a spreadsheet, row by row, marking "PASS" or "FAIL" manually.
  4. For failures, raises a separate Jira ticket and waits for another team to remediate.
  5. Returns to the same server 3–5 days later to verify remediation, SSHs in again, re-runs the command, re-updates the spreadsheet.
  6. Compiles the 62 per-server spreadsheets into one master report — usually a weekend exercise.
  7. QSA visits, requests additional evidence not in the spreadsheet, triggering another round of manual SSH sessions.

🛠️ 7. Automated Solution Design

The solution has two distinct phases per run: SCAN (assess, never change) → REMEDIATE (fix auto-remediable findings) → RESCAN (prove fixes worked) → REPORT (generate QSA-grade evidence).

  • SCAN phase: Uses OpenSCAP (oscap CLI driven by Ansible) against the RHEL9 CIS Benchmark XCCDF profile to scan every CDE server and collect a pass/fail result set without touching any configuration.
  • REMEDIATE phase: Dedicated Ansible tasks fix only the pre-approved auto-remediable control categories (SSH hardening, password policy, auditd config, unnecessary services). Any other finding is written to the exceptions list and raised as a ServiceNow incident — never auto-remediated.
  • RESCAN phase: Re-runs the OpenSCAP scan post-remediation to prove the auto-fixes worked — a critical step for QSA credibility ("we don't just claim we fixed it; we prove it").
  • REPORT phase: Collects SCAP HTML/XML reports from all hosts, archives them to the compliance NFS share, and generates an executive summary CSV with before/after scores, all attached to the AAP workflow notification sent to InfoSec and the QSA's secure portal.
  • The entire workflow is also scheduled (every Sunday at 2 AM IST) for continuous compliance, not just pre-audit runs.

  • Job Templates: PCI-Scan-CDE, PCI-Remediate-CDE, PCI-Rescan-CDE, PCI-Report-CDE
  • Workflow Template: PCI-DSS-Compliance-Pipeline (chaining all 4 phases with approval before remediate)
  • Schedule: Weekly Sunday 02:00 IST, pre-audit on-demand
  • Survey: scan_profile (cis-l2 / pci-dss), auto_remediate (bool), raise_servicenow (bool)
  • Notification Template: Email compliance summary to CISO, InfoSec, QSA portal upload webhook
  • RBAC: PCI-Remediate-CDE requires "PCI-Compliance-Engineers" team membership to launch

💻 12. Complete Project Code (Line-by-Line Explained)

Role: scap_scan

roles/scap_scan/tasks/main.yml
---
- name: Ensure OpenSCAP scanner and SCAP Security Guide are installed
  ansible.builtin.dnf:
    name:
      - openscap-scanner
      - scap-security-guide
    state: present

- name: Create SCAP results directory on remote host
  ansible.builtin.file:
    path: "{{ scap_results_dir }}"
    state: directory
    owner: root
    group: root
    mode: '0700'

- name: Run OpenSCAP XCCDF evaluation against CIS/PCI-DSS profile
  ansible.builtin.command: >
    oscap xccdf eval
    --profile {{ scap_profile }}
    --results {{ scap_results_dir }}/results-{{ ansible_date_time.iso8601_basic_short }}.xml
    --report  {{ scap_results_dir }}/report-{{ ansible_date_time.iso8601_basic_short }}.html
    --fetch-remote-resources
    {{ scap_datastream }}
  register: scap_output
  changed_when: false
  failed_when: scap_output.rc not in [0, 2]

- name: Extract overall compliance score from SCAP XML results
  ansible.builtin.shell: >
    grep -oP '(?<=score=")[^"]+' {{ scap_results_dir }}/results-{{ ansible_date_time.iso8601_basic_short }}.xml | head -1
  register: scap_score_raw
  changed_when: false

- name: Store pre-remediation score as host fact
  ansible.builtin.set_fact:
    pre_remediation_score: "{{ scap_score_raw.stdout | default('0') | float | round(2) }}"
    scap_report_timestamp: "{{ ansible_date_time.iso8601_basic_short }}"

- name: Extract list of FAILED rules from SCAP XML
  ansible.builtin.shell: >
    grep -A1 'result>fail' {{ scap_results_dir }}/results-{{ ansible_date_time.iso8601_basic_short }}.xml |
    grep 'idref' | grep -oP 'idref="\K[^"]+' | sort -u
  register: failed_rules_raw
  changed_when: false

- name: Store failed rules as host fact list
  ansible.builtin.set_fact:
    scap_failed_rules: "{{ failed_rules_raw.stdout_lines | default([]) }}"

- name: Fetch HTML report to control node for archiving
  ansible.builtin.fetch:
    src: "{{ scap_results_dir }}/report-{{ scap_report_timestamp }}.html"
    dest: "{{ scap_reports_local }}/{{ inventory_hostname }}/pre-remediation-report.html"
    flat: true

- name: Fetch XML results to control node for compliance archive
  ansible.builtin.fetch:
    src: "{{ scap_results_dir }}/results-{{ scap_report_timestamp }}.xml"
    dest: "{{ scap_reports_local }}/{{ inventory_hostname }}/pre-remediation-results.xml"
    flat: true
Line-by-Line Explanation
oscap xccdf eval — The core OpenSCAP command. xccdf eval means "evaluate an XCCDF benchmark" (as opposed to oval eval or cpe eval — different evaluation types). This is the industry-standard tool recognized by QSAs as objective evidence, unlike ad-hoc shell commands.
--profile {{ scap_profile }} — Selects the specific compliance profile within the datastream. We use the CIS Level 2 Server profile (cis_server_l2_rhel9) which maps almost exactly to PCI-DSS v4.0 requirements. Alternative: use the dedicated PCI-DSS profile (pci-dss) in the SSG package — in banks, the QSA typically specifies which profile to use in the initial scoping call.
--results ... .xml and --report ... .html — Two output formats: the XML is machine-parseable (Ansible reads it back to extract scores/failed rules), the HTML is human-readable (QSA and management can open it in a browser). Generating both in one command is a PCI-DSS best practice.
failed_when: scap_output.rc not in [0, 2] — OpenSCAP returns RC 0 for full pass, RC 2 for "scan completed but failures found" (the normal outcome on first scan), RC 1 for actual scan errors. Without this, Ansible would mark every partially-failing scan as an Ansible task failure, aborting all subsequent plays — we want to detect findings AND continue to remediate them.
ansible.builtin.fetch with flat: true — Pulls a file from each remote host down to the Ansible control node (or EE workspace in AAP). flat: true puts the file directly into the named directory without adding a hostname sub-folder automatically (we handle sub-folder naming in the dest path ourselves for better control).
scap_failed_rules fact — A list of XCCDF rule IDs that failed (e.g. xccdf_org.ssgproject.content_rule_sshd_disable_root_login). The remediation roles check their specific rule IDs against this list to decide whether to remediate — no unnecessary remediation on already-passing controls.

Role: pci_ssh_hardening

roles/pci_ssh_hardening/tasks/main.yml
---
- name: Skip SSH hardening if not in auto-remediation mode
  ansible.builtin.meta: end_host
  when: not auto_remediate | bool

- name: Deploy PCI-DSS compliant sshd_config from template
  ansible.builtin.template:
    src: sshd_config_pci.j2
    dest: /etc/ssh/sshd_config.d/99-pci-hardening.conf
    owner: root
    group: root
    mode: '0600'
    validate: '/usr/sbin/sshd -t -f %s'
  notify: Reload sshd

- name: Ensure SSH root login is disabled (belt-and-suspenders check)
  ansible.builtin.lineinfile:
    path: /etc/ssh/sshd_config
    regexp: '^PermitRootLogin'
    line: 'PermitRootLogin no'
    state: present
    backup: true
  notify: Reload sshd

- name: Ensure SSH password authentication is disabled
  ansible.builtin.lineinfile:
    path: /etc/ssh/sshd_config
    regexp: '^PasswordAuthentication'
    line: 'PasswordAuthentication no'
    backup: true
  notify: Reload sshd

- name: Check for any unauthorized UID-0 accounts beyond root
  ansible.builtin.shell: "awk -F: '($3==0 && $1!=\"root\") {print $1}' /etc/passwd"
  register: extra_uid0
  changed_when: false

- name: Flag unauthorized UID-0 accounts for manual review (never auto-delete)
  ansible.builtin.debug:
    msg: "⚠️ FINDING: Unauthorized UID-0 account(s) detected on {{ inventory_hostname }}: {{ extra_uid0.stdout_lines }} — raising ServiceNow incident"
  when: extra_uid0.stdout_lines | length > 0

- name: Raise ServiceNow incident for unauthorized UID-0 accounts
  ansible.builtin.uri:
    url: "{{ servicenow_api_url }}"
    method: POST
    headers:
      Content-Type: "application/json"
      Authorization: "Basic {{ vault_snow_api_token | b64encode }}"
    body_format: json
    body:
      short_description: "PCI-DSS FINDING: Unauthorized UID-0 account on {{ inventory_hostname }}"
      description: "Accounts: {{ extra_uid0.stdout_lines | join(', ') }} detected during PCI compliance scan. Manual review required."
      category: "Security"
      priority: "2"
      assigned_to: "infosec-compliance"
  delegate_to: localhost
  when:
    - extra_uid0.stdout_lines | length > 0
    - raise_servicenow | bool

PubkeyAuthentication yes
AuthorizedKeysFile .ssh/authorized_keys
PermitEmptyPasswords no
X11Forwarding no
MaxAuthTries 4
LoginGraceTime 60
ClientAliveInterval 900
ClientAliveCountMax 0
AllowAgentForwarding no
AllowTcpForwarding no
Banner /etc/issue.net
Ciphers aes256-gcm@openssh.com,aes128-gcm@openssh.com,aes256-ctr,aes192-ctr,aes128-ctr
MACs hmac-sha2-512-etm@openssh.com,hmac-sha2-256-etm@openssh.com
KexAlgorithms curve25519-sha256,ecdh-sha2-nistp521,diffie-hellman-group14-sha256
Line-by-Line Explanation
dest: /etc/ssh/sshd_config.d/99-pci-hardening.conf — On RHEL 8.7+ and RHEL 9, sshd supports a sshd_config.d/ drop-in directory. We write our PCI config as a numbered drop-in (99- prefix ensures it loads last and takes precedence) rather than editing the main sshd_config directly — this is far less invasive and easier to audit/revert.
validate: '/usr/sbin/sshd -t -f %s' — Before replacing the file, Ansible first runs sshd -t -f <tempfile> to syntax-validate the new config. If the template has a typo that would create an invalid sshd config, this catches it BEFORE the old config is overwritten — preventing an "SSH lockout" on production CDE servers. One of the most important production safety patterns in SSH management.
ClientAliveInterval 900 / ClientAliveCountMax 0 — Enforces a 15-minute idle SSH session timeout. PCI-DSS Requirement 8.2.8 mandates that idle sessions time out within 15 minutes — 900 seconds = exactly 15 minutes.
Ciphers / MACs / KexAlgorithms — Restricts SSH to only FIPS-140-2 / NIST-approved algorithms (AES-256, SHA-2 family, ECDH P-521, Curve25519). Older algorithms like arcfour, MD5, DH-group1 are explicitly excluded — a PCI-DSS Req 4.2.1 requirement for strong cryptography in transit.
Raise ServiceNow incident for UID-0 accounts — This is the key distinction between auto-remediable (wrong config value) and human-review-required findings (unexpected privileged account). Ansible never auto-deletes user accounts — it flags them to the right team via ServiceNow, satisfying PCI-DSS Req 8.6.1 without taking an irreversible destructive action automatically.

Role: pci_password_policy

roles/pci_password_policy/tasks/main.yml
---
- name: Skip if not in auto-remediation mode
  ansible.builtin.meta: end_host
  when: not auto_remediate | bool

- name: Deploy PCI-DSS compliant pwquality.conf from template
  ansible.builtin.template:
    src: pwquality.conf.j2
    dest: /etc/security/pwquality.conf
    owner: root
    group: root
    mode: '0644'
    backup: true

- name: Enforce password maximum age (PCI-DSS Req 8.3.9 — 90-day rotation)
  ansible.builtin.lineinfile:
    path: /etc/login.defs
    regexp: '^PASS_MAX_DAYS'
    line: 'PASS_MAX_DAYS   90'

- name: Enforce password minimum age (prevent rapid cycling to reuse old password)
  ansible.builtin.lineinfile:
    path: /etc/login.defs
    regexp: '^PASS_MIN_DAYS'
    line: 'PASS_MIN_DAYS   1'

- name: Enforce minimum password length in login.defs
  ansible.builtin.lineinfile:
    path: /etc/login.defs
    regexp: '^PASS_MIN_LEN'
    line: 'PASS_MIN_LEN    14'

- name: Enforce account lockout after 6 failed attempts (PCI-DSS Req 8.3.4)
  ansible.builtin.lineinfile:
    path: /etc/security/faillock.conf
    regexp: '^deny'
    line: 'deny = 6'
    create: true

- name: Enforce 30-minute lockout duration after account lockout
  ansible.builtin.lineinfile:
    path: /etc/security/faillock.conf
    regexp: '^unlock_time'
    line: 'unlock_time = 1800'
roles/pci_password_policy/templates/pwquality.conf.j2
# PCI-DSS v4.0 Req 8.3.6 — Password complexity — Managed by Ansible
minlen = 14
minclass = 4
maxrepeat = 3
dcredit = -1
ucredit = -1
ocredit = -1
lcredit = -1
difok = 4
gecoscheck = 1
dictcheck = 1
Line-by-Line Explanation
minlen = 14 — PCI-DSS v4.0 Requirement 8.3.6 mandates a minimum of 12 characters for passwords; CIS Benchmark Level 2 (our chosen profile) raises this to 14. We use 14 to satisfy both simultaneously without dual maintenance.
minclass = 4 — Requires characters from all 4 classes (uppercase, lowercase, digit, special) — equivalent to the old dcredit=-1 ucredit=-1 ocredit=-1 lcredit=-1 individually, but set as a single class-count constraint.
difok = 4 — At least 4 characters in the new password must differ from the old one, preventing trivial one-character-bump cycling (e.g., changing Password2025! to Password2026!).
faillock.conf deny = 6 — After 6 consecutive failed authentication attempts the account is locked. PCI-DSS Req 8.3.4 mandates lockout after "not more than 10 invalid access attempts" — we set 6 as a more conservative bank-appropriate value.
unlock_time = 1800 — 1800 seconds = 30 minutes lockout duration, satisfying PCI-DSS Req 8.3.4's requirement that the account "remain locked for a minimum of 30 minutes or until an administrator resets the account."

Line-by-Line Explanation
-D (first rule) — Deletes/clears ALL existing audit rules before loading the new set. Without this, rerunning the playbook appends duplicate rules, which inflates the kernel audit buffer and can cause performance issues. Always start auditd rule files with -D.
-b 8192 — Sets the in-kernel audit backlog buffer to 8192 entries. The default (320 on RHEL) is too small for busy CDE servers handling hundreds of sudo events per minute; a full buffer causes the kernel to either drop records or halt the system (depending on -f setting) — both are PCI violations.
-w /etc/sudoers -p wa -k privilege_escalation — -w = watch this file/directory; -p wa = on write or attribute-change events; -k privilege_escalation = tag matching events with this key so ausearch -k privilege_escalation finds them instantly during incident investigation. PCI-DSS Req 10.2.3 requires logging all use of privileged access mechanisms.
{% for rule in auditd_extra_rules | default([]) %} — Injects tier-specific rules from group_vars (e.g., syscall-level execve logging on DB servers) without forking a separate template per server tier — one template, configurable per group via variables.
-e 2 (last rule, "make rules immutable") — Locks the audit ruleset so it cannot be modified at runtime without a reboot. This is the most important PCI-DSS Req 10.5.2 control: an attacker who gains root cannot silently disable audit logging mid-attack. The trade-off: Ansible also cannot modify rules without a reboot, so rule changes trigger a scheduled-maintenance reboot (acceptable in a bank's change process).
admin_space_left_action = halt — When disk space for audit logs reaches a critical low, the system HALTS. This is a PCI-DSS Req 10.7 requirement that audit logging must never silently stop — the bank would rather have a controlled halt than run with unaudited activity. This setting requires careful disk capacity planning (covered via the patching pre-check pattern from Project 2).

      - talk

  ansible.builtin.set_fact:

  ansible.builtin.systemd:
    name: "{{ item }}"
    state: stopped
    enabled: false
  loop: "{{ all_prohibited_services }}"
  register: service_disable_result
  failed_when: false

- name: Mask prohibited services so they cannot be accidentally re-started
  ansible.builtin.systemd:
    name: "{{ item }}"
    masked: true
  loop: "{{ all_prohibited_services }}"
  failed_when: false

- name: Check for any world-writable files in critical system paths
  ansible.builtin.command: "find /etc /usr/bin /usr/sbin -type f -perm -002"
  register: world_writable_files
  changed_when: false

- name: Flag world-writable system files for manual review (never auto-chmod)
  ansible.builtin.debug:
    msg: "⚠️ FINDING: World-writable files found on {{ inventory_hostname }}: {{ world_writable_files.stdout_lines }}"
  when: world_writable_files.stdout_lines | length > 0
Line-by-Line Explanation
masked: true on systemd services — "Masking" goes beyond simply disabling a service: it symlinks the unit file to /dev/null, making it impossible for any process, user, or dependency to start it — even accidentally. PCI-DSS Req 2.2.4 requires not just "stopped" but "cannot be started" for prohibited services in the CDE.
failed_when: false on both service tasks — Many prohibited services (telnet, rsh, etc.) aren't installed at all on modern RHEL servers; systemd returns an error for non-existent service units. We suppress these errors because "telnet isn't installed" is actually a PASS state — we just don't want Ansible to abort the play over it.
pci_blocked_services | default([]) — Merges the global prohibited list with any tier-specific additions (e.g., the DB tier group_vars blocks httpd/nginx/tomcat). The default([]) prevents a variable-undefined error on hosts that don't have tier-specific additions.
World-writable files check with find /etc /usr/bin /usr/sbin -perm -002 — Deliberately flagged but NOT auto-remediated (no chmod task follows). Automatically chmod o-w-ing files in /usr/sbin could break package manager integrity checks and is irreversible without testing — this is a human-review finding for the security team.

Design Rationale
Three separate playbooks (scan / remediate / report) map directly to three separate AAP Job Templates in the Workflow, giving the team precise control over which phase to re-run. If remediation is complete but the report NFS mount was temporarily unavailable, only pci_report.yml needs to be re-run — not the full scan+remediate cycle. Separating phases also means the scan playbook can run in "read-only" mode (no become-required changes) with a different, narrower privilege level for audit-only runs.

🔄 14. Execution Flow — Start to Finish

1
The AAP Scheduled Job triggers at Sunday 02:00 IST (or Suresh manually launches pre-audit). AAP pulls the latest playbook code from the SecureBank GitLab project (auto-sync enabled).
2
The EE container (with openscap-scanner + scap-security-guide pre-installed) spins up on an Execution Node. AAP injects Machine credentials and Vault credentials.
3
Ansible facts are gathered for all 62 CDE hosts in parallel (forks=62). ansible_kernel, ansible_date_time, and OS facts are collected automatically — used in report generation.
4
Phase 1 (Scan): oscap xccdf eval runs remotely on each host via the command module, generating per-host XML+HTML SCAP reports. ansible.builtin.fetch pulls them to the EE workspace/NFS.
5
Failed XCCDF rule IDs are extracted with grep from the XML and stored as a host fact list (scap_failed_rules) — visible in the AAP job output for the approver to review.
6
Workflow pauses at the Approval Node. The InfoSec lead reviews the scan output in the AAP UI — pre-remediation scores visible per host — and approves remediation.
7
Phase 2 (Remediate): SSH hardening template deployed (with sshd -t validation), pwquality.conf written, auditd rules deployed (with -e 2 immutability), prohibited services stopped and masked. Each in serial 25% batches.
8
Any high-risk findings (UID-0 accounts, world-writable system files, SIEM unreachable) are automatically raised as ServiceNow incidents via the uri module calling the ServiceNow REST API — all delegate_to: localhost to route from the control node.
9
Phase 3 (Report): Post-remediation SCAP scan re-runs, capturing improved scores. Per-host compliance_record facts are built with pre/post scores, delta, and AAP job ID.
10
The Jinja2 executive summary template (run_once) aggregates all 62 hosts' compliance_records from hostvars into one timestamped CSV and copies it to the NFS compliance archive.
11
Workflow notification fires — Slack message with average compliance score improvement, email to InfoSec with the CSV attached and a link to the AAP job output (the full audit trail).
12
Any remaining open findings (ServiceNow tickets) are tracked through standard ITSM change management, with the next weekly Sunday scan automatically verifying remediation.

🎯 16. Interview Preparation

Scenario-Based Questions

Q: How do you differentiate between findings you auto-remediate and findings requiring human review?
We pre-classify findings into two categories: configuration-value findings (wrong sshd parameter, missing auditd rule, incorrect pwquality setting) are auto-remediable because the correct value is fully defined by policy and the fix is reversible. Existence/access findings (unexpected UID-0 account, unknown open port, world-writable system file) require human judgment because the "fix" (delete an account, close a port) may have unintended consequences — these are flagged via ServiceNow incidents instead.
Q: A QSA asks: "How do I know the SCAP report you showed me wasn't generated before you fixed the problem?" How do you answer?
The SCAP report filename and embedded timestamp shows when it was generated. The AAP job ID embedded in the compliance CSV links directly to the AAP Activity Stream, which shows the full job timeline — scan started at T, remediation ran T+15m, rescan ran T+45m, report generated T+60m. The QSA can independently log into the AAP UI with read-only access and verify the entire sequence, including which user launched the job.
Q: How do you handle a CDE server that's completely offline during the compliance scan?
Unreachable hosts are recorded in the Ansible play recap as "unreachable=1" and do not appear in the compliance CSV (the hostvars guard in the Jinja2 template handles this). A separate post-play task (not shown) queries ansible_play_batch against the inventory count and raises a ServiceNow ticket for every host that didn't produce a compliance_record — these become "evidence gap" incidents requiring manual investigation before the QSA audit.

Production Support & Architecture Questions

Q: Why use OpenSCAP instead of writing your own custom Ansible checks for each PCI control?
Three reasons: (1) OpenSCAP and the SCAP Security Guide are NIST-validated tools — QSAs accept their output as objective evidence without questioning the methodology; hand-rolled shell checks require the QSA to independently validate your assessment logic. (2) SSG is maintained by Red Hat and the compliance community — 200+ controls updated with every RHEL release. (3) XCCDF/HTML output is a standardized, auditor-familiar format; a CSV of "ok: [hostname]" lines from an Ansible play is not.
Q: How would you extend this to Windows servers in the CDE?
The same Workflow structure applies — swap OpenSCAP for Microsoft Security Compliance Toolkit or the Windows-specific SSG SCAP content. AAP's WinRM-based Windows inventory support means the same Workflow can chain a Linux SCAP scan job and a Windows SCAP scan job in parallel branches, with a single consolidated report at the end — giving the CISO a unified cross-platform compliance score.
Q: What is the -e 2 auditd flag and why is it important for PCI-DSS?
-e 2 sets the audit system to "locked" mode — the ruleset becomes immutable until the next reboot. No process, including root, can delete or modify audit rules at runtime. PCI-DSS Req 10.5.2 requires protecting audit logs and log-generating systems from modification. Without -e 2, an attacker who gains root during an incident can auditctl -D (delete all rules) and operate silently — this single line defeats that attack vector entirely.

📝 17. Key Learnings, Mistakes & Resume Points

Key Learnings

  • OpenSCAP + SCAP Security Guide is the industry-standard toolchain for PCI-DSS evidence — QSAs explicitly prefer it over ad-hoc shell scripts.
  • Separating SCAN (read-only) from REMEDIATE (changes) from REPORT into three independent playbooks/job templates is essential for audit flexibility and minimizing blast radius.
  • The distinction between auto-remediable (config value) and human-review-required (existence/access) findings is the most important design decision in any compliance automation project.
  • Auditd -e 2 (immutable ruleset) is a PCI-critical hardening step that most junior engineers miss entirely.

Common Mistakes

  • Skipping the sshd -t validate step — a bad template locks engineers out of CDE servers, a major incident requiring console/iDRAC access.
  • Auto-remediating findings like unexpected user accounts — deleting accounts can break application service users.
  • Not capturing pre-remediation state — without a pre-scan baseline, you can't prove to the QSA that you improved anything.
  • Forgetting the hostvars guard in Jinja2 aggregation templates — crashes the report for offline hosts.

Production Best Practices

  • Schedule compliance scans weekly, not just before audits — continuous compliance is the only sustainable posture for PCI-DSS Level 1.
  • Embed the AAP job ID in every compliance record — creates an independently verifiable audit chain that QSAs accept without additional evidence.
  • Keep a separate "scan-only" RBAC role for junior engineers and auditors — scan without remediation privilege is safe for any team member to run.
  • Use sshd_config.d/ drop-ins instead of editing the main sshd_config — vastly reduces the risk of SSH lockout from template errors.

Resume Points

  • Architected and implemented a PCI-DSS v4.0 continuous compliance automation pipeline using Ansible AAP and OpenSCAP across 62 Cardholder Data Environment servers, replacing 18 engineer-days of manual audit preparation with a fully automated weekly scheduled job.
